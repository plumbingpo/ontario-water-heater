Most tankless problems announce themselves the same way, with a blinking code on the display and a shower that turns cold. Behind that code is a system with very little margin for error. There is no storage tank to fall back on, so the heater has to detect flow, light a burner or power its elements, and raise the water temperature by fifty degrees or more in the few seconds it takes water to cross the heat exchanger. When scale narrows the passages, when the flow sensor stops reading, when the flame rod cannot confirm a flame, or when the vent or gas line cannot keep up, the unit protects itself by shutting down. That is the pattern we see again and again in Ontario, CA.

Ontario Water Heater starts every tankless repair by reading the unit, not guessing at it. On every service call in Ontario, we pull the active error code and the stored fault history, then test the components that code points to: flow sensor output while a faucet runs, flame rod signal in microamps, igniter resistance, inlet gas pressure both static and at full fire, and vent condition from the unit to the termination. Scale gets checked by comparing inlet and outlet temperatures against flow rate, since a heat exchanger that cannot transfer heat gives itself away in those numbers. A code tells us where to look, not what to replace. An ignition code, for example, can come from a worn igniter, a dirty flame rod, low gas pressure, or a blocked intake, and each one calls for a different fix.

Symptoms That Mean It's Time to Call

Recognizing the warning signs early can save you money and keep a slow drip from turning into a flooded utility room. If any of the following sound familiar, it's worth having the unit checked:

  • Repeated ignition attempts, then a shutdown
  • Unusual noises from the cabinet, like a whine, a roar, or a rattle
  • A heater that only fires when water runs at full blast
  • Temperature dropping sharply when a second shower or the dishwasher starts
  • An error code flashing on the unit's display
  • Moisture, rust, or drips appearing below the heater
  • Water that swings between hot and lukewarm in the middle of a shower
  • Longer bursts of cold water between hot draws than you used to get

Why Hire a Professional for Tankless Water Heater Repair?

Start with what is at stake. A gas-fired tankless heater can burn nearly 200,000 BTU per hour at full fire, and its vent is the only thing carrying carbon monoxide out of the house; a joint that pulls loose or an intake that gets blocked changes that in a hurry. On the electric side, the loads are large, frequently more than 100 amps spread over several 240-volt circuits. Then there is the diagnostic puzzle. Error codes point to a system, not a part, so a flow sensor fault, a scaled heat exchanger, and a bad thermistor can look nearly identical on the display, and guessing gets expensive.

Calling a pro often costs less than trial and error. Our technicians do not guess at error codes. They test flow sensors with water moving, measure flame rod signal, confirm igniter resistance, read inlet gas pressure at full fire, and check the vent from end to end. When scale is the problem, we flush it out properly and rinse the system before the unit goes back in service. And because some tankless problems, like an undersized gas line or a cracked heat exchanger, are not solved by parts at all, we tell you so directly, before you commit to anything.

Tips to Keep Your Water Heater Healthy

A little routine care goes a long way toward keeping a water heater running well past its expected lifespan. Here's where to begin:

  • Write down any error code before resetting the unit, and call if it keeps coming back
  • Get the supply line checked before adding gas appliances, which can starve a tankless unit
  • Clean the inlet water filter screen a few times a year so the flow sensor gets full flow
  • Clear debris, snow, and dryer lint away from the vent and intake openings
  • Protect the heat exchanger with a softener or scale inhibitor when your water is hard
  • Never cut power to an outdoor or garage unit in winter, because it needs electricity to protect itself from freezing
  • Have the unit descaled at least once a year, or more often if your water is hard
