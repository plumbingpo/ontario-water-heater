Getting More Years From Your Water Heater in Fontana

Many early failures start with skipped maintenance, and the habits that prevent them are easy. Drain and flush the tank once a year to clear sediment, have the anode rod inspected every few years, and test the relief valve annually so it doesn't seize. Keep the temperature near 120 degrees to reduce scald risk and slow scale buildup. If you have a tankless unit, descale it yearly where the water is hard to protect the heat exchanger.

Take a look at the age of your water heater. The serial number on the label usually includes the month and year it was built, and that date tells you a lot. Most tank models make it to 8 to 12 years, heat pump units 10 to 15, and regularly descaled tankless units around 20. If your heater is past that range and also shows discolored water, rumbling, or moisture at the base, it's usually due for a planned replacement, which is almost always cheaper and less stressful than an emergency replacement after a leak.

If warning signs do show up, acting quickly keeps them small. A dripping relief valve might point to high water pressure or a missing expansion tank, both inexpensive fixes. The same drip ignored for months can keep stressing the tank until a seam gives way, and a small repair becomes a full replacement and a cleanup. It's smart to keep our number saved on your phone so you can reach us at the first sign of trouble. Early intervention saves time, money, and cold showers.

What Goes Wrong With Local Water Heaters

Water heaters in and around Fontana face a predictable set of stresses. Depending on the neighborhood and the water source, hard water may be part of the picture: calcium and magnesium drop out as sediment on the tank floor, forcing the burner or lower element to work harder, and that's what causes the popping and rumbling you may hear when the heater fires. Tankless units aren't immune either: scale builds inside the heat exchanger, and that's why regular descaling matters so much in hard-water areas.

We also see a lot of aging equipment. A standard tank water heater typically lasts 8 to 12 years, and near the end of that window the anode rod has often been eaten away, leaving the tank exposed to corrosion. Homes here also run on a mix of fuel types, natural gas and propane units in some homes and electric in others, which means we service both sides: thermocouples, burners, and venting on gas units, and elements, thermostats, and breakers on electric ones.

Code is another piece. Replacing an older heater often means updating the installation along the way: a thermal expansion tank if the water system is closed, a relief valve drain line piped to an approved spot, venting that drafts properly on gas units, plus straps and a pan where required. Well water brings its own challenges, including extra sediment, iron staining, and the rotten-egg smell that occasionally develops when certain water chemistry reacts with a standard anode rod. Every one of these problems is fixable, and most cost far less to address early.
